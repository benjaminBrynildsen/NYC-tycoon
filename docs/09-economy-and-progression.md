# 08 — Economy & Progression

## The real antagonist

Not a rival. **Debt.**

Real estate is a leveraged business: you buy a $10M building with $3M of your
own money and $7M of someone else's, and that asymmetry is the entire source of
both the fortunes and the ruin. The game should be unambiguous that leverage is
the protagonist's drug.

### The capital stack
Every project is financed, and structuring the stack is a real decision:

- **Equity** — yours. Expensive (it's finite), total control.
- **Senior debt** — a bank. Cheapest money, strictest covenants, first in line.
  Loan-to-cost ratio is the dial: more leverage, more return, less margin for
  error.
- **Mezzanine / preferred** — expensive gap money that lets you do a deal you
  can't quite afford. Where over-reach lives.
- **Partners** — outside equity. Cheap, dilutive, and *they have opinions*:
  a partner can force a sale at the worst moment, or block a concession you
  wanted to make.
- **Seller financing / ground lease** — creative structures that get you into
  deals with no money, at the cost of long-term flexibility.

### Covenants
The mechanism that turns debt into drama. Loans carry conditions: a debt service
coverage ratio, a completion deadline, a leasing milestone, a reputational
clause. **Breaching a covenant hands the lender power** — they can raise the
rate, demand cash, freeze draws, or foreclose. A covenant breach is how a bad
quarter becomes an emergency, and it's how [Heat](07-politics-and-approvals.md#heat)
becomes financial.

### Cash flow vs. value
The two numbers, and the tension between them is the whole game of real estate:

- **Value** is what your portfolio is worth on paper, and it goes up in booms
  whether or not you're any good.
- **Cash** is what's in the account on the 1st, when debt service, taxes,
  payroll, insurance, and maintenance are due.

You go broke on cash while rich on value. The game must make this happen to
players and must make it *legible* — a value-rich, cash-poor player should be
able to see the crunch coming three months out and have real, painful choices:
sell an asset, refinance at a worse rate, defer maintenance (with street-level
consequences), take a partner, or break a promise.

## The cycle

A multi-year boom/bust cycle, partly visible, never precisely predictable.
Drivers: interest rates, capital availability, employment, construction costs,
and a sentiment term that overshoots in both directions.

The design requirement: **the cycle must be readable in advance by a skilled
player and punishing to a greedy one.** Leading indicators exist and are
diegetic — cap rate compression, land prices outrunning rents, everyone breaking
ground at once, cranes visible from your roof, a rival paying a number that
doesn't make sense, your banker suddenly eager. A player who learns to read the
skyline instead of the ledger is playing the game correctly.

A bust is not just a number going down. It is: your lease-up stalls, your
construction loan matures into a market with no refinancing, your partner wants
out, your rival goes under and their half-built site sits there rusting for two
years — and **everything cheap is for sale to whoever has cash.** Surviving a
bust with dry powder is the single most powerful move in the game.

## The three meters

Deliberately three, deliberately named, deliberately in permanent tension.

### CAPITAL
Liquidity + borrowing capacity. What you can do *now*.

### STANDING
The sum of trust held by people and institutions. Reduces approval time and
opposition, opens off-market deals, unlocks rezonings, cools Heat, attracts good
staff. Built slowly by kept promises and physical presence; lost quickly and
publicly.

### HEAT
Adversarial attention. See [07](07-politics-and-approvals.md#heat).

**The tension:** almost every meaningful action trades one for another.
Fast money raises Heat. Building Standing costs Capital and, more scarcely,
*time on the street*. Spending Standing to push a project through works, and
spends it. The strategic identity of a playthrough is the shape of that
triangle over time.

## The five tiers

Progression is about **scope of agency**, not a skill tree. Each tier opens a
new system and a new camera relationship to the city.

### Tier 1 — The Building (opening ~3–5 hrs)
One walk-up in one neighborhood. You know every tenant by name. You fix the
boiler yourself because you can't afford a plumber. There is no board layer
worth speaking of — a paper map and a notebook. **This tier is the tutorial for
pillar 2**: you learn the city through your feet, so that later, when the board
starts telling you things, you already know what the board leaves out.

### Tier 2 — The Block (~10 hrs)
Three to six buildings. Your first hire, your first lie from an employee, your
first renovation, your first Community Board meeting, your first bank loan. The
Overseer view unlocks — and it should feel like a revelation, because you've
earned the ability to see the pattern you've been walking through.

### Tier 3 — The Site (~20 hrs)
Your first assemblage and your first ground-up building. The full Vision tool,
the full approvals ladder, a real org, real leverage, the first serious rival
conflict, and a construction site you will visit at night. **This is the core
game.** The tiers before it are onboarding; the tiers after are escalation.

### Tier 4 — The District (~30 hrs)
Multiple concurrent projects, a rezoning you pursue for a whole corridor,
infrastructure deals with the city, a portfolio to manage through a full market
cycle. You can no longer attend everything; triage and delegation become the
game. Tier-3 Model Room.

### Tier 5 — The Skyline (endgame)
A megaproject: a platform over rail yards, a waterfront, a neighborhood invented
whole. Citywide politics. You are now one of the exogenous forces
([08](08-simulation-core.md#exogenous-forces)) acting on everybody else. The
Model Room is a room you walk around inside, and out its window is the actual
city, and you can point to the things you made.

**The final beat is a walk.** The game's ending — whatever the outcome — should
put you back on the sidewalk at Tier 1's address, on foot, at street level, in
the neighborhood you started in, to look at what it became. No stats screen
until after. That's the game.

## Failure

Not a game over screen. Failure states are positions you can play out of, or
not:

- **Insolvency** — lose assets to lenders, fall a tier, keep your relationships
  (or don't). Bankruptcy is survivable and thematically correct.
- **Exposure** — Heat resolves against you; Standing floors out; certain
  neighborhoods and certain people are closed to you permanently. A run can
  become geographically constrained, which is a fascinating way to lose.
- **Irrelevance** — the quiet one. You play safe, the cycle passes you, rivals
  reshape the city, and you own a nice stable portfolio in a city you no longer
  have any influence over. The game should let this happen without comment.

## Scoring

There is no score. There is **the ledger** ([10](10-people-and-story.md#the-ledger)):
what you built, what you demolished, who's still here, who isn't, what you
promised, and what you kept. Presented at the end without judgment, as a list of
places and people, most of which you can go stand in front of.
